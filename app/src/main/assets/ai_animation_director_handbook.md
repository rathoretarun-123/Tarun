PROFESSIONAL TECHNICAL DIRECTION HANDBOOK

A practical operating manual for directing original, emotionally engaging, visually refined AI-generated animated films.

Creative reference: storytelling craft, expressive character acting, cinematic staging, and production discipline associated with leading Disney and Pixar animated features.

Version 1.0 • October 2026

How to Use This Handbook

This handbook is an operating guide, not a guarantee that any particular AI tool will produce flawless footage. The Director should plan, generate, review, correct, and approve shots before they enter the final edit. Use it alongside the screenplay, character bible, storyboard, audio assets, and the capabilities of the selected tools.

The governing standard

Do not treat animation as a sequence of attractive images. Every shot must advance the story, reveal character, establish space, create emotion, or deliver essential information.

Five governing principles

1.  Emotion drives technique: camera, lighting, acting, and music support the dramatic objective.

2.  Performance drives attention: the audience should understand what a character is thinking, including during silence.

3.  Continuity creates credibility: identity, costume, geography, props, lighting, and screen direction remain stable.

4.  Restraint creates sophistication: avoid unnecessary camera movement, exaggerated expressions, and visual effects.

5.  Sequence quality matters more than isolated beauty: judge shots in the context of the edited scene.

Chapter 1 — The Director’s Mandate

Responsibilities

-   Narrative clarity, dramatic pacing, and emotional progression.

-   Character motivation, acting, gestures, expressions, and eye direction.

-   Visual design, composition, cinematography, lighting, color, and materials.

-   Dialogue, voice performance, lip-sync, sound design, and music.

-   Spatial continuity, prop continuity, costume continuity, and screen direction.

-   Review, correction, approval, and final quality control.

Originality

Use broad filmmaking principles as references, not instructions to reproduce a studio’s proprietary characters, signature designs, exact scenes, or distinctive film identity. Build an original visual language: character silhouettes, facial design, materials, environments, palettes, and emotional rhythm.

Chapter 2 — Screenplay Analysis

Required pre-production documents

6.  Logline, synopsis, central dramatic question, and ending.

7.  Character biographies, objectives, fears, flaws, and relationships.

8.  Scene-by-scene breakdown and emotional arc.

9.  Location, prop, costume, and recurring visual-motif lists.

10. Dialogue and silence plan, including important reactions.

11. Major reveals, reversals, climax, and resolution.

12. Preliminary runtime, sequence breakdown, and shot budget.

Scene objective

For every scene, document the narrative objective, each character’s immediate goal, the obstacle, the emotional change, the visual idea, and the exit condition. A scene should not exist solely to showcase an attractive environment.

Dramatic diagnostic

Use this flexible sequence where useful: Establish → Orient → Desire → Obstacle → Reaction → Decision → Consequence. It is a diagnostic tool, not a rigid formula.

Example

Two princesses discover a mysterious doorway in an abandoned palace. Establish the location; orient the audience to their positions; show one sister’s curiosity and the other’s fear; allow a glance or silence to communicate disagreement; show the decision to open the door; end on a consequence that launches the next beat.

Narrative integrity

Do not casually alter essential plot information, relationships, motivations, or the ending. Document significant proposed changes and evaluate them against the complete story.

Chapter 3 — Character Bible and Visual Identity

Master specification

-   Identity: name, role, age range, personality, defining facial features, eye and hair design, skin tone, and distinctive marks.

-   Anatomy: head-to-body ratio, body proportions, height, posture, gait, balance, hands, and habitual movement.

-   Costume: garment layers, fabric, embroidery, seams, fasteners, jewelry, footwear, palette, and approved variations.

-   Performance: resting face, listening face, speech style, emotional range, characteristic gestures, and movement quality.

-   Continuity restrictions: features that must not change unless the story explicitly requires it.

Master-sheet views

-   Front, three-quarter, side, and back views.

-   Full-body views and height comparisons.

-   Neutral face and expression sheet.

-   Gesture, posture, hand, and movement references.

-   Costume, accessory, color, and material details.

Identity lock

Keep facial proportions, eye shape and spacing, iris color, hairstyle, body proportions, costume silhouette, signature accessories, and apparent age stable. Expressions can change; the underlying identity should not.

Consistency protocol

13. Use the approved master reference.

14. Specify the exact costume variant, pose, orientation, and expression.

15. Use reference conditioning or character-reference features when available.

16. Compare the generated shot with the master sheet.

17. Reject or correct meaningful identity drift before continuing.

Text prompts alone cannot guarantee perfect identity consistency. Reference images, stable settings where available, and manual review are essential.

Chapter 4 — Art Direction and Visual Development

Visual constitution

Approve a visual development document before final shot production. Define stylization, character proportions, facial language, architecture, material treatment, palette, lighting philosophy, camera language, atmospheric effects, texture detail, and motion quality.

Environment bible

-   Establishing views and architectural layout.

-   Entrances, exits, landmarks, and character paths.

-   Furniture, recurring props, surfaces, and background activity.

-   Time-of-day, weather, lighting, and atmospheric variants.

-   Scale references and camera-friendly angles.

Depth and composition

Use foreground, midground, and background when they help establish depth and direct attention. Keep the frame simple when intimacy or emotional clarity calls for it.

Color script

Map palette and contrast across the film. Warmth may support familiarity; restrained saturation may support mystery; contrast may intensify conflict; a distinctive palette may define the climax. These are options, not fixed emotional rules. Preserve continuity across adjacent shots.

Chapter 5 — Cinematography and Shot Design

Five questions for every shot

18. What must the audience see?

19. What should the audience feel?

20. Where should attention go first?

21. What information is revealed or concealed?

22. Why is this camera position preferable to alternatives?

Shot-size vocabulary

-   Extreme wide shot: Scale, geography, isolation, or spectacle.

-   Wide shot: Movement and character-environment relationships.

-   Full shot: Whole-body action and posture.

-   Medium shot: Dialogue, gestures, and interaction.

-   Medium close-up: Facial acting with some body language.

-   Close-up: A significant expression, realization, or decision.

-   Extreme close-up: A meaningful detail such as fingers, eyes, or an object.

-   Over-the-shoulder: Conversational relationship and viewpoint.

-   Insert: An important object, clue, or physical action.

-   Point of view: What a character sees.

Camera angles

-   Eye level: natural, neutral, or intimate observation.

-   Low angle: power, threat, scale, or aspiration when context supports it.

-   High angle: vulnerability, exposure, or spatial comprehension.

-   Overhead: patterns, geography, choreography, or isolation.

-   Dutch angle: deliberate instability; use sparingly.

-   Profile and three-quarter angles: silhouette, relationships, and facial volume.

Lens and perspective

As approximate full-frame virtual-camera starting points: 24–35 mm for broad environments; 35–50 mm for general coverage; 50–85 mm for intimate portraits; 85 mm and above for compressed backgrounds and isolated details. Camera distance and sensor size also affect perspective. Avoid accidental facial distortion.

Motivated movement

-   Dolly in: intimacy, discovery, realization, or tension.

-   Dolly out: context, isolation, consequences, or distance.

-   Pan or tilt: follow action or reveal relevant information.

-   Tracking: maintain a relationship with moving action.

-   Crane: reveal geography or scale.

-   Orbit: show a changing relationship or pivotal moment.

-   Static frame: let acting, tension, and composition carry the scene.

Do not move the camera simply to make a shot feel expensive.

Screen direction

For dialogue, establish the axis between characters. Record screen positions, gaze directions, camera positions, and entrances/exits. Respect the 180-degree principle unless a deliberate transition establishes a new orientation.

Dialogue coverage

A possible sequence is an establishing two-shot, medium interaction, over-the-shoulder coverage, a close-up at an important beat, a listener reaction, and a return to a two-shot when the relationship changes. This is an option, not a formula. Sustained two-shots can be more effective than repetitive shot-reverse-shot editing.

Chapter 6 — Character Acting and Emotional Performance

Performance hierarchy

23. Motivation.

24. Thought process.

25. Attention and eye direction.

26. Body posture.

27. Gesture.

28. Facial expression.

29. Dialogue delivery.

30. Fine facial and secondary movement.

Thought before action

Direct the sequence Stimulus → Perception → Interpretation → Decision → Action → Reaction. A character who hears a sound may pause, look toward it, listen, turn, and then decide whether to approach. Keep these beats motivated and proportionate.

Eye direction

Specify the target, direct or peripheral gaze, eye-contact duration, gaze breaks, and timing relative to head movement. Avoid vacant staring and random eye movement.

Facial animation

Coordinate eyebrows, eyelids, eyes, cheeks, mouth, jaw, and head angle. Use subtle asymmetry when appropriate. Avoid generic smiles, constant eyebrow motion, and exaggerated expressions without dramatic justification.

Body language

Weak direction: “The princess looks worried.”

Actionable direction: “The princess stops halfway through her step. Her shoulders rise slightly as she inhales. Her right hand tightens around her shawl. Her eyes shift toward the dark doorway before her head turns. She holds still, listening.”

Timing

Use anticipation, main action, follow-through, overlapping movement, secondary motion, and settling where appropriate. Stillness and delayed reactions can be as important as movement.

Chapter 7 — Lighting, Materials, and Rendering

Lighting plan

-   Key light: defines principal form.

-   Fill: controls shadow density.

-   Rim or edge light: separates a subject from the background.

-   Practical light: a visible source in the environment.

-   Ambient illumination: establishes the overall environment.

-   Atmospheric light: supports volumetric depth when justified.

Continuity record

Record time of day, key direction, source softness, shadow direction, warm-cool relationship, background illumination, exposure, contrast, atmospheric density, and practical-light positions. Camera changes must remain consistent with the location.

Material behavior

-   Skin: controlled specular response and stylization-appropriate shading.

-   Hair: consistent silhouette and coherent highlights.

-   Fabric: plausible folds, thickness, roughness, and movement.

-   Metal: distinct reflections and controlled highlights.

-   Stone: scale-appropriate surface variation.

-   Glass: coherent transparency, reflection, refraction, and thickness.

Finishing

Maintain stable exposure, color management, readable shadows, controlled highlights, appropriate motion blur, consistent depth of field, and restrained bloom. Watch for flicker, temporal instability, oversharpening, and excessive gloss.

Chapter 8 — AI Shot-Generation Pipeline

Production sequence

Script analysis → Shot design → Reference preparation → Prompt creation → Generation → Review → Correction → Approval → Edit integration.

Reference hierarchy

31. Approved character master references.

32. Approved costume references.

33. Approved environment references.

34. Previously approved shots from the same scene.

35. Lighting and color references.

36. Storyboard or composition reference.

An earlier frame containing an error must not override the approved character or environment bible.

Shot-prompt architecture

-   Project identity: title, scene ID, shot ID, duration, aspect ratio, frame rate.

-   Character identity: approved reference and costume variant.

-   Environment: location, time, geography, props, and background.

-   Dramatic intention: what the shot communicates and how emotion changes.

-   Blocking: start position, movement path, interactions, end position.

-   Performance: expression, gaze, gesture, body mechanics, speech or silence.

-   Cinematography: shot size, angle, perspective, movement, focus, composition.

-   Lighting and look: source direction, palette, materials, atmosphere.

-   Motion and constraints: timing, secondary movement, continuity requirements, exclusions.

Example production prompt

SCENE SC04_SH07 — 6 seconds. Original high-end stylized 3D animated feature-film aesthetic, refined materials, restrained character acting, cinematic lighting, and coherent perspective.

Character: Use the approved Princess A master reference and exact blue embroidered costume. Preserve facial proportions, eye color, hairstyle, height, jewelry, and garment construction.

Environment: Established eastern palace corridor at night. Preserve stone columns, arched windows, carved wooden door, floor pattern, and lantern placement.

Dramatic intention: The princess hears something unexpected and is uncertain whether to investigate.

Performance: She walks at a relaxed pace, hears the sound, pauses, and shifts her eyes toward the doorway before turning her head. Her eyebrows tighten slightly, lips part, and she takes a quiet breath. No dialogue.

Camera: Medium-wide three-quarter view at approximately eye level. A subtle dolly-in begins after the pause. Preserve screen direction and spatial relationships.

Lighting: Warm lantern light from camera-left, cool moonlight through the windows, soft contact shadows, restrained background separation.

Motion: Natural walking, clear pause, eye movement preceding head movement, subtle cloth settling.

Continuity: No costume changes, identity drift, extra fingers, altered architecture, spontaneous props, unexplained lighting changes, camera teleportation, or abrupt screen-direction changes.

End state: The princess is still, looking toward the doorway, body angled slightly away from it. Leave a brief beat for the next shot.

Negative constraints

When supported, exclude recurring defects such as character redesign, costume changes, malformed hands, floating objects, inconsistent shadows, unintended text, random background characters, uncontrolled camera movement, abrupt pose changes, and mouth movement during designated silence. Use targeted constraints rather than indiscriminate lists.

Chapter 9 — Storyboarding and Animatics

Storyboard requirements

-   Shot number and framing.

-   Camera position and character positions.

-   Action and emotional beat.

-   Dialogue, sound, and approximate duration.

-   Transition to the next shot.

Animatic review

Assemble storyboard panels with temporary dialogue and sound. Review story comprehension, scene rhythm, shot duration, entrances and exits, reaction timing, dialogue clarity, emotional buildup, repetition, and runtime. Remove a beautiful shot if it weakens the sequence.

Timing

Do not give every shot the same duration. A brief physical reaction may be very short; an emotional realization may require several seconds. Refine timing in the complete sequence.

Chapter 10 — Hindi Voice Acting, Dialogue, and Lip-Sync

Voice identity bible

Specify perceived age, tonal quality, vocal range, pace, accent, diction, breathiness or vocal weight, emotional range, speech habits, and pronunciation of names. Avoid giving every character the same generic voice.

Hindi dialogue preparation

37. Finalize dialogue in Devanagari.

38. Confirm meaning and emotional subtext.

39. Identify pronunciation-sensitive words.

40. Mark pauses, interruptions, and emphasis.

41. Establish character-specific rhythm.

42. Review dialogue against the screenplay.

Prefer natural Hindi syntax and idiomatic speech. Match vocabulary to the character and fictional culture; avoid unnatural literal translation.

Performance notation example

Dialogue: “तुम यहाँ… अकेली क्या कर रही हो?”

Direction: Quiet suspicion mixed with concern. Begin softly, pause after “यहाँ”, slightly emphasize “अकेली”, and finish with restrained upward inflection. Eyes narrow slightly; head inclines forward; body remains still.

Phoneme-aware lip-sync

43. Finalize spoken audio before detailed lip-sync.

44. Segment audio into utterances.

45. Obtain word-level or phoneme-level timing when available.

46. Map phonemes to the mouth shapes supported by the animation system.

47. Coordinate jaw opening, lip closure, lip rounding, cheeks, and facial expression.

48. Review frame by frame and at normal playback speed.

49. Check synchronization in the complete edited scene.

Hindi includes vowels, aspirated consonants, retroflex sounds, and other articulations that must be represented as well as the available rig allows. Do not force English mouth-shape assumptions onto every Hindi phoneme.

Listening shots

During another character’s dialogue, animate listening behavior rather than continuous lip motion. Use attentive gaze, small reactions, breathing, and motivated posture changes. Keep the mouth at rest unless the character is actually speaking or reacting.

Audio continuity

Maintain consistent voice identity, loudness, room acoustics, microphone character, background noise, and reverberation within the same location. Check pronunciation and emotional continuity across edits.

Chapter 11 — Editing, Sound, and Music

Editing principles

-   Cut when the story, action, emotion, or attention benefits from the transition.

-   Preserve action direction and spatial understanding.

-   Allow important reactions to register.

-   Avoid repeating information already communicated clearly.

-   Use transitions that fit the film’s tone.

-   Review pacing in the complete sequence, not just individual clips.

Sound design

Build a layered soundscape: dialogue, ambience, Foley, designed effects, and music. Keep important dialogue intelligible. Sound effects must correspond to visible actions and physical space.

Music

Music should reinforce, contrast, or deepen the scene—not continuously instruct the audience what to feel. Leave space for silence, environmental sound, and acting.

Final mix review

-   Dialogue intelligibility and consistent loudness.

-   No unintended clipping, distortion, or abrupt ambience changes.

-   Music does not mask important speech.

-   Effects have believable spatial placement.

-   Transitions and endings feel deliberate.

Chapter 12 — Continuity Management

Continuity ledger

Maintain a record for every scene covering:

-   Character positions and orientations.

-   Costume and accessory states.

-   Prop ownership and placement.

-   Injuries, dirt, wetness, and other state changes.

-   Time of day and lighting.

-   Environmental damage or changes.

-   Screen direction and eye-lines.

-   Dialogue state and emotional progression.

-   Start and end state of each shot.

Shot-to-shot continuity

Compare every new shot with the preceding and following shots. Check hand position, prop state, body orientation, gaze, costume details, background landmarks, lighting, and action timing. A shot can be visually impressive and still fail continuity.

Chapter 13 — Quality Assurance and Approval

Four approval gates

Gate 1 — Story

Does the shot communicate the intended information and emotional beat?

Gate 2 — Performance

Are motivation, gaze, gesture, expression, timing, and physical behavior convincing?

Gate 3 — Visual and technical

Are identity, composition, lighting, materials, anatomy, motion, and image stability acceptable?

Gate 4 — Continuity and edit

Does the shot connect coherently to adjacent shots in geography, timing, sound, and emotional progression?

Quality scoring

Score each category from 1 to 5. Use scores as a review aid, not as a substitute for judgment.

-   Narrative clarity: ____ / 5

-   Emotional effectiveness: ____ / 5

-   Character identity consistency: ____ / 5

-   Acting and motion: ____ / 5

-   Cinematography and composition: ____ / 5

-   Lighting and materials: ____ / 5

-   Anatomical and temporal stability: ____ / 5

-   Audio and lip-sync: ____ / 5

-   Spatial and prop continuity: ____ / 5

-   Integration with the sequence: ____ / 5

Any critical failure in identity, story clarity, continuity, or dialogue synchronization requires correction regardless of the average score. A high average must not hide a serious defect.

Chapter 14 — Failure Diagnosis and Correction

-   Character face changes: Strengthen reference conditioning, reduce unnecessary design descriptors, and compare against the approved master sheet.

-   Costume changes between shots: Use costume-specific references and a locked costume description.

-   Unmotivated camera movement: Rewrite the shot objective and simplify the camera path.

-   Generic emotional acting: Specify thought, gaze, gesture, timing, and the emotional transition.

-   Unstable architecture: Use location references, fixed landmarks, and consistent blocking.

-   Incorrect lip-sync: Use finalized audio, timing data where available, and manual review.

-   Overly glossy surfaces: Specify roughness, highlight control, and material-specific behavior.

-   Chaotic movement: Reduce simultaneous actions and direct one primary movement at a time.

-   Inconsistent lighting: Reuse the scene lighting specification and compare adjacent shots.

-   Slow or repetitive editing: Review the animatic and remove shots that add no new information or emotion.

Correction rule

Identify the primary failure, correct that failure, and regenerate or revise only what is necessary. Do not change character design, camera, lighting, and performance simultaneously unless a full rework is justified.

Appendix A — Master Prompt for the AI Director

You are the AI Animation Director responsible for the artistic and technical integrity of an original animated feature film. Direct the production as a coherent cinematic work, not as a collection of isolated images.

Before generating a shot, understand its narrative purpose, character objective, emotional beat, environment, continuity dependencies, camera plan, lighting, movement, and audio requirements. Use approved character and location references whenever supported. Preserve the visual constitution and continuity ledger.

For each shot, define the shot ID, duration, starting state, ending state, character identity, costume, environment, blocking, performance, camera, lighting, motion constraints, and relationship to adjacent shots. Generate, review, correct, and approve before integrating the shot into the edit.

Prioritize story clarity, believable acting, refined composition, consistent visual identity, motivated camera movement, and restrained effects. Do not imitate proprietary characters or reproduce specific copyrighted scenes. Build an original visual identity informed by general principles of excellent animation filmmaking.

When a result fails, identify the main defect and make a targeted correction. Never assume a text prompt guarantees perfect output. If a requirement cannot be achieved by the current tool, state the limitation and propose a practical workflow workaround.

Final Directive

The standard is not “make every frame beautiful.” The standard is “make every frame serve the film.” Refinement emerges from the interaction of storytelling, acting, composition, motion, sound, continuity, and disciplined revision.

The Director must be willing to simplify, hold a shot longer, remove unnecessary movement, regenerate flawed footage, and reject attractive images that do not serve the story.
